  8: % ========== Abstract ==========
  9: \begin{abstract}
 10: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a large language model (LLM) answer from external evidence, but every paragraph sent to the LLM costs tokens, and irrelevant paragraphs can lower answer quality. Multi-hop questions make this harder, because later evidence is often only weakly related to the question itself. This work studies \emph{cheap evidence selection}: a small cross-encoder (22.7M parameters) decides which paragraphs the LLM sees.
 11: 
 12: On HotpotQA, a fine-tuned small selector sent 68--77\% fewer prompt tokens than the ten retrieved paragraphs, with small answer-F1 differences whose 95\% confidence intervals included zero. On MuSiQue, ranking quality rather than the stopping rule appeared to limit cost, and re-scoring the remaining paragraphs with the question plus the paragraphs already selected (iterative reranking) seemed to help; a pair-matched control showed that part of that gain came from additional training data. The weight difference that lets the selector use such a conditioned query was functionally approximated by a per-matrix rank-2 graft: without it the matched model collapses when run iteratively, and the graft recovered 86.8\% of the gap (95\% CI $[77.7\%, 95.8\%]$), against 7--9\% for norm-matched random controls. Overall, the grafted model did not beat one-shot ranking; any advantage is expected on long chains. On 566 unused four-hop questions, conditioning-trained iteration improved full-support recall@5 by 0.055 (95\% CI $[0.007, 0.102]$), and on questions with three or more hops its evidence improved the reader's answer F1 by 0.076 (95\% CI $[0.041, 0.112]$) at the same five-paragraph budget; a router that predicts hop count did not yield a useful cascade.

  1: \chapter{Introduction}
  2: \label{chap:intro}
  3: 
  4: \section{Context}
  5: 
  6: Large language models store knowledge in their parameters, but this \emph{parametric} knowledge is hard to update, hard to attribute and prone to errors \cite{pmlr-v119-guu20a,3495724.3496517,liu2026unifieddefinitionhallucinationits}. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) adds a \emph{non-parametric} memory: a retriever finds passages, and the LLM conditions its answer on them \cite{3495724.3496517,pmlr-v119-guu20a}. This makes answers easier to update and to verify, but it moves part of the cost to the prompt. Every passage sent to the LLM is paid for in input tokens, and passages that do not support the answer can mislead the model \cite{liu2024lostinthemiddle,wang2023filco}.
  7: 
  8: Multi-hop questions make this trade-off sharper. In a question such as ``In which city was the founder of the company that makes product $X$ born?'', the second and third pieces of evidence share little vocabulary with the question; they are related to the evidence found in the previous step \cite{yang2018hotpotqa,trivedi2022musique}. A selector that scores each passage against the question alone tends to rank such \emph{bridge} passages low, so a system either sends many passages, which is expensive, or risks losing the chain.
  9: 
 10: \section{Problem and research questions}
 11: 
 12: This work studies \emph{cheap evidence selection} for multi-hop RAG: a small model, much cheaper than the LLM, decides which retrieved paragraphs the LLM sees. The cost of interest is the LLM's input tokens, plus the selector's own compute, measured as cross-encoder forward passes. The questions are:
 13: 
 14: \begin{description}
 15:   \item[RQ1] How many prompt tokens can a small fine-tuned cross-encoder save at equal evidence recall and answer quality?
 16:   \item[RQ2] On harder multi-hop data, is the limit the stopping rule (how many passages to send) or the ranker itself?
 17:   \item[RQ3] Does conditioning the ranker on the passages already selected (iterative reranking) help, once confounds such as the amount of training data are controlled?
 18:   \item[RQ4] What is the structure of the weight change that gives a small reranker this chain-following ability, and can it be isolated as a small, reusable component?
 19: \end{description}
 20: 
 21: \section{Hypothesis}
 22: 
 23: The central hypothesis is that \textbf{chain-following in a small reranker can be isolated as a low-rank weight change that matters mainly for long reasoning chains}. If it holds, the change can be trained and shipped as a small adapter on top of a cheap one-shot selector and switched on only where it helps, keeping short questions cheap. The preliminary results support the first part functionally (by grafting an extracted change); training the adapter directly is part of the remaining work.
 24: 
 25: \section{Contributions so far}
 26: 
 27: \begin{enumerate}
 28:   \item An evaluation of a 22.7M-parameter cross-encoder as an evidence selector: token accounting at fixed recall targets and answer quality with an LLM reader on HotpotQA and MuSiQue.
 29:   \item Evidence that, on MuSiQue, the one-shot ranker rather than the stopping rule limits cost, and a pair-matched control showing that part of the apparent benefit of iterative reranking came from additional training data.
 30:   \item Evidence that the weight change behind the ability to use a conditioned query is functionally approximated at rank 2 (about 85\% of its effect recovered, random controls 7--9\%), and pre-registered tests showing that conditioning-trained iteration helps long chains, in evidence recall and in answer F1.
 31:   \item A research protocol for an AI-assisted project: pre-registration of every decision rule in version control, paired statistics, adversarial review of each protocol before running it, and a claim ledger that records null results (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
 32: \end{enumerate}
 33:

 35: 
 36: Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces RAG, multi-hop question answering, cross-encoder reranking and the structure of fine-tuning updates. Chapter~\ref{chap:related} positions this work against related research. Chapter~\ref{chap:method} describes the models, data, inference procedures, analyses and the research protocol.   1: \chapter{Background}
  2: \label{chap:background}
  3: 
  4: This chapter introduces the concepts the rest of the text relies on: retrieval-augmented generation, multi-hop question answering, cross-encoder reranking, the structure of fine-tuning updates, and the evaluation metrics and statistical tests used.
  5: 
  6: \section{Parametric and non-parametric knowledge}
  7: 
  8: A Transformer language model \cite{vaswani2017attention} stores what it learned in its weights. This \emph{parametric} knowledge is implicit: it cannot be inspected or updated piece by piece, and the model may produce fluent but unsupported statements, which \citeonline{liu2026unifieddefinitionhallucinationits} analyse as failures of the model's world model.
  9: 
 10: Retrieval-augmented models add a \emph{non-parametric} memory. REALM \cite{pmlr-v119-guu20a} trains a retriever jointly with a masked language model; RAG \cite{3495724.3496517} conditions a generator on passages from a dense retriever. In the systems studied here the LLM is used as a fixed reader: a retriever returns candidate paragraphs, a selector chooses some of them, and the LLM answers from the selected ones.
 11: 
 12: \section{Multi-hop question answering}
 13: 
 14: A question is \emph{multi-hop} when its answer requires combining several pieces of evidence. HotpotQA \cite{yang2018hotpotqa} provides two-hop questions with ten candidate paragraphs each, two of them supporting and eight distractors. MuSiQue \cite{trivedi2022musique} composes two- to four-hop questions from single-hop ones and filters out questions that can be answered by shortcuts; each question has about twenty candidate paragraphs. In both datasets the supporting paragraphs are annotated, so evidence recall can be measured without an LLM. 2WikiMultiHopQA \cite{ho2020twowiki} is a third, related benchmark planned for the remaining work.
 15: 
 16: \section{Cross-encoder reranking}
 17: 
 18: A \emph{bi-encoder} embeds the question and each paragraph separately and compares the embeddings, which is fast but loses interactions between them \cite{reimers2019sentence}. A \emph{cross-encoder} reads the question and the paragraph together and outputs a single relevance score \cite{nogueira2019passage}. It is more accurate but needs one forward pass per (question, paragraph) pair. This work uses \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2}, a six-layer MiniLM \cite{wang2020minilm} cross-encoder with 22.7M parameters trained for passage reranking on MS MARCO \cite{bajaj2016msmarco}. Scoring the twenty paragraphs of a MuSiQue question takes twenty forward passes.
 19: 
 20: \textbf{One-shot and iterative reranking.} A one-shot reranker scores every paragraph against the question once and sorts them. An \emph{iterative} reranker selects one paragraph at a time and re-scores the remaining ones with a query that contains the question and the paragraphs already selected. Iterative selection can follow a chain from one piece of evidence to the next, at the cost of more passes \cite{xiong2021mdr,zhang2024beamretrieval}.
 21: 
 22: \section{Structure of fine-tuning updates}
 23: 
 24: Fine-tuning changes a pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ into $W_1 = W_0 + \Delta$. Several lines of work show that useful updates are often well approximated in a low-dimensional subspace. \citeonline{aghajanyan2021intrinsic} measure a low intrinsic dimension of fine-tuning, and LoRA \cite{hu2022lora} trains only a low-rank update $\Delta = BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ and small $r$. Given a full update, its best rank-$r$ approximation in the Frobenius norm is the truncated singular value decomposition,
 25: \begin{equation}
 26: \Delta_r = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top,
 27: \label{eq:svd}
 28: \end{equation}
 29: where $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$ are the singular values of $\Delta$ and $u_i, v_i$ the corresponding singular vectors (the Eckart--Young theorem). PHLoRA \cite{vasani2025phlora} uses this operation to extract adapters from fine-tuned checkpoints. Task vectors, the differences between fine-tuned and base weights, can also be added or subtracted to transfer behaviour between models \cite{ilharco2023taskarithmetic,yadav2023ties}.
 30: 
 31: \section{Metrics}
 32: 
 33: \begin{description}
 34:   \item[Full-support recall@$k$.] The fraction of questions whose top-$k$ selected paragraphs contain \emph{all} supporting paragraphs. A question with three supporting paragraphs counts only if all three are selected. This is stricter than the usual per-paragraph recall, and matches what a reader needs to answer a multi-hop question.
 35:   \item[Tokens.] The number of input tokens of the reader's prompt with the selected paragraphs, counted with the reader's tokenizer (\texttt{o200k\_base}) or as reported by the API.
 36:   \item[Passes.] Cross-encoder forward passes per question, the selector's compute cost.
 37:   \item[Answer F1 and EM.] Token-level F1 and exact match between the reader's answer and the reference answer (maximum over aliases), after standard normalization.
 38: \end{description}
 39: 
 40: \section{Statistical tests}
 41: 
 42: All comparisons are paired: two systems are evaluated on the same questions. For binary per-question outcomes (for example, whether all supporting paragraphs are in the top five), the exact McNemar test uses only the discordant questions \cite{mcnemar1947}. For differences in means (tokens, F1), 95\% confidence intervals are obtained with the paired bootstrap over questions \cite{efron1979bootstrap}. These choices follow the recommendations of \citeonline{dror2018hitchhiker}. A non-significant difference is not evidence of equivalence \cite{card2020power}; the later experiments therefore test ``no worse'' claims as non-inferiority with a margin fixed in advance, while the early ones report the interval only.

===== 03_related_work.tex =====
  1: \chapter{Related Work}
  2: \label{chap:related}
  3: 
  4: This chapter positions the work against three lines of research: context selection for RAG, multi-hop retrieval, and the structure of fine-tuning updates. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarizes what is known and what this work adds.
  5: 
  6: \section{Context selection and compression for RAG}
  7: 
  8: Several methods reduce what the LLM reads. RECOMP \cite{xu2024recomp} trains compressors that summarize retrieved documents; LongLLMLingua \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua} prunes tokens; FILCO \cite{wang2023filco} filters context at the span level; Provence \cite{chirkova2025provence} prunes sentences with a trained cross-encoder; and CompAct \cite{yoon2024compact} compresses iteratively. Adaptive methods decide how much to retrieve: Self-RAG \cite{asai2024selfrag} and Adaptive-RAG \cite{jeong2024adaptiverag} use the LLM or a classifier to decide, DynamicRAG \cite{sun2025dynamicrag} learns the number of documents, and Adaptive-$k$ \cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} cuts the ranked list at the largest gap in similarity scores and adds a buffer of extra passages (five by default). Noise-Gate \cite{lavado2027noisegate}, the author's earlier work, selects paragraphs by embedding-similarity thresholds and reported 26--27\% fewer tokens on HotpotQA; in that study Adaptive-$k$ selected too few passages and lost answer quality. Listwise LLM rerankers \cite{sun2023rankgpt,pradeep2023rankzephyr} are accurate but far more expensive than a small cross-encoder.
  9: 
 10: Compared with this line, the present work does not propose a new compression mechanism. Its practical contribution is a careful cost accounting of a very small selector at matched recall and answer quality, and the finding that on harder multi-hop data the ranker, not the stopping rule, is the bottleneck.
 11: 
 12: \section{Multi-hop retrieval}
 13: 
 14: Multi-hop dense retrieval \cite{xiong2021mdr} and Baleen \cite{khattab2021baleen} retrieve iteratively, conditioning each step on what was found before. IRCoT \cite{trivedi2023ircot} interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning by an LLM, and EfficientRAG \cite{zhuang2024efficientrag} replaces some LLM calls with small models. Beam Retrieval \cite{zhang2024beamretrieval} is the closest method to the iterative reranker studied here: a cross-encoder scores each candidate together with the question and the passages already chosen, with beam search. It reports high support recall on MuSiQue with much larger encoders (DeBERTa base and large).
 15: 
 16: The iterative reranker in this work is therefore not a new method. What is new is (i) its evaluation at 22.7M parameters with a pair-matched control that separates the effect of conditioning from the effect of more training data, and (ii) the analysis of the weight change it requires.
 17: 
 18: \section{Structure of fine-tuning updates}
 19: 
 20: The low intrinsic dimension of fine-tuning \cite{li2018intrinsic,aghajanyan2021intrinsic} and the success of low-rank adapters \cite{hu2022lora} are well established. PHLoRA \cite{vasani2025phlora} extracts low-rank adapters from full fine-tuning checkpoints with truncated SVD, the same operation used here. Localization studies find that task skills concentrate in few parameters or layers \cite{panigrahi2023skilllocalization}, including the middle layers of a large reranker \cite{perlman2026emergence}. LoRA updates differ structurally from full fine-tuning \cite{biderman2024loralearnsless,shuttleworth2025illusion}, and rank measures of updates need a noise baseline before they are interpreted \cite{altaf2026noisebaseline}.
 21: 
 22: Behaviours, not only domains, can sit in very low-rank updates. Rank-1 adapters suffice to induce emergent misalignment in an LLM, and the induced direction transfers across fine-tunes \cite{turner2025modelorganisms,soligo2025convergent}. Reasoning gains from reinforcement learning can be recovered with tiny adapters, while supervised fine-tuning needs much larger updates \cite{morris2026tinylora,ye2026rlvrlowrank}. Task arithmetic transfers domain knowledge between rerankers \cite{braga2025taskarithmetic}.
 23: 
 24: Against this background, a low-rank fine-tuning update is not surprising in itself. The specific claim tested here is narrower: a \emph{retrieval behaviour} (using already-selected evidence in the query) in a small reranker is a rank-2 change, isolated with a pair-matched control and norm-matched random controls, and it pays off on long chains.

  1: \chapter{Materials and Methods}
  2: \label{chap:method}
  3: 
  4: \section{Data}
  5: 
  6: \textbf{HotpotQA} (distractor setting) is used with the same 400 test questions as \citeonline{lavado2027noisegate} and 1,000 disjoint development questions, fixed by identifier. Each question has ten candidate paragraphs, two of them supporting.
  7: 
  8: \textbf{MuSiQue-Ans} \cite{trivedi2022musique} is used for the multi-hop experiments. The official test labels are not public, so all evaluation sets are drawn from the labelled development split, disjoint from each other and fixed in advance:
  9: \begin{itemize}
 10:   \item a 400-question test set and a 300-question tuning set (\texttt{dev300}), used up to the matched-control experiment;
 11:   \item a \emph{fresh} set of all 1,017 development questions not used before (529, 322 and 166 two-, three- and four-hop), used for the confirmation experiment.
 12: \end{itemize}
 13: Training questions come from the MuSiQue training split: 1,000 questions for fine-tuning and further disjoint samples for fitting stopping rules and routers. Every split was written to a file and committed before any model was scored on it.
 14: 
 15: \section{Models}
 16: 
 17: All selectors are the same 22.7M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder; only the weights differ (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Training uses a pointwise binary cross-entropy loss: each training example is a (query, paragraph) pair labelled 1 if the paragraph is supporting and 0 otherwise. Fine-tuning on MuSiQue uses AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 16, one epoch and a maximum length of 512 tokens; when the input is too long, the query side is truncated (if a paragraph alone exceeds the limit, both sides are truncated).

 36: 
 37: \textbf{The confound and its control.} M1-iter sees about 2.2 times as many training pairs as M1, so a comparison of the two mixes the effect of conditioning with the effect of more training. M1-ctrl removes this confound: it receives the same paragraphs, labels, order, number of steps and learning-rate schedule as M1-iter, and differs only in the query text. M1-iter and M1-ctrl were trained with three seeds each.
 38: 
 39: \section{Inference}
 40: 
 41: \textbf{One-shot.} Every paragraph is scored against the question and the list is sorted; this costs one pass per paragraph (about 20 on MuSiQue).
 42: 
 43: \textbf{Iterative.} For four greedy steps, the remaining paragraphs are scored against a query made of the question followed by the paragraphs selected so far (most recent first, separated by a special token), and the highest-scoring paragraph is selected. The remaining paragraphs are then ordered by their scores in the last step. This costs about $20+19+18+17 = 74$ passes per question.
 44: 
 45: \textbf{Adaptive stopping.} Instead of a fixed $k$, a logistic regression estimates, for each $k$, the probability that the top $k$ contains all supporting paragraphs, from features of the selector's scores (the sorted scores, the score at position $k$ and $k{+}1$, and their gap). The system sends the smallest $k$ whose probability exceeds a threshold $\tau$; $\tau$ is chosen on a tuning set as the value with the fewest tokens that reaches a recall target (0.90 or 0.95), and applied once to the test set.
 46: 
 47: \section{Delta grafting}
 48: 
 49: To study the structure of a fine-tuning update, the difference between two models is \emph{grafted}, partly, onto the first one. Given a base model with weights $\{W^{(b)}\}$ and a target model $\{W^{(t)}\}$, the delta of each weight matrix is $\Delta = W^{(t)} - W^{(b)}$. A rank-$r$ graft replaces every two-dimensional $\Delta$ by its truncated SVD $\Delta_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:svd}) and keeps one-dimensional parameters (biases, layer norms) in full. A layer graft adds the full delta of a single Transformer layer only. The effect of a graft $g$ is summarized by its \emph{recovery},
 50: \begin{equation}
 51: \mathrm{rec}(g) = \frac{R(g) - R(\text{base})}{R(\text{target}) - R(\text{base})},
 52: \label{eq:recovery}
 53: \end{equation}
 54: where $R$ is full-support recall@5 under the target's inference mode. As a control, each $\Delta_r$ is replaced by a random rank-$r$ matrix with the same Frobenius norm; if random matrices recovered as much, ``low-rank'' would be indistinguishable from ``any perturbation of that size''. The graft is chosen without looking at data, so the tuning set can be used to select the smallest rank with at least 80\% recovery, which is then confirmed once on a held-out set with a paired bootstrap confidence interval for the recovery ratio.
 55: 
 56: \section{Research protocol}
 57: \label{sec:protocol}
 58: 
 59: Every experiment was pre-registered: its question, data splits, metrics, decision rules and budget were committed to version control before any model was scored on evaluation data \cite{nosek2018preregistration}, and deviations were recorded. Each experiment has one primary test; exploratory results are reported as such and are not promoted to confirmed claims without a new registered test on unused data. Recent protocols also state the minimum detectable effect \cite{card2020power}. Because the project uses AI tools for coding, analysis and drafting, each protocol was reviewed before running by an independent model instructed to argue for the null hypothesis, every citation was checked against its official source, and results copied between machines were recomputed and compared. Code, protocols, results and a ledger of all claims are kept in the project repository.

 29: \section{Adaptive stopping saves further tokens}
 30: \label{sec:res_adaptive}
 31: 
 32: On HotpotQA, choosing between two and four paragraphs per question with a learned stopping rule saved 57 and 71 tokens per question against A1's threshold at recall targets 0.90 and 0.95 (CIs $[-71, -44]$ and $[-91, -53]$), with recall differences not significant. On MuSiQue, adaptive stopping beat the pre-registered fixed-$k$ policy of the iterative ranker at recall 0.95 ($-244$ tokens, CI $[-296, -192]$) but not at 0.90 ($-43$, CI $[-95, +9]$), and the iterative ranker's scores were not a better stopping signal than one-shot scores (a refuted secondary). Descriptively, adaptive stopping on the one-shot in-domain ranker reached recall 0.95 with 1,554 tokens and 20 passes per question, against 2,535 tokens for sending all paragraphs. Adaptive-$k$ \cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} with its default buffer of five paragraphs, applied to the same cross-encoder scores, spent the same tokens as the learned rule but recovered fewer complete chains (0.815 vs 0.868; exploratory, test set); without the buffer it cut after 3.7 paragraphs on average (recall 0.50). This agrees with its failure on cosine scores in \citeonline{lavado2027noisegate}.
 33: 
 34: \section{On MuSiQue, ranking quality limits cost}
 35: \label{sec:res_bottleneck}
 36: 
 37: With A1 (trained on HotpotQA), the top ten paragraphs contained all supporting paragraphs for only 68\% of MuSiQue questions, so no stopping rule limited to $k\le10$ could reach the planned recall targets; the pre-registered comparison was declared not testable. The results below, from later experiments, suggest that ranking quality is the main limiting factor. Fine-tuning A1 on 1,000 MuSiQue questions (M1) raised full-support recall@5 from 0.355 to 0.682, and reduced the tokens needed for recall 0.90 from 1,957 to 1,533. The iterative model M1-iter reached 0.750 (McNemar $p=0.0067$ against M1), with the largest gain on four-hop questions (0.58 vs 0.36). That comparison, however, was confounded by the amount of training data (Section~\ref{sec:res_matched}).
 38: 
 39: \section{The iteration change is functionally rank 2}
 40: \label{sec:res_rank}
 41: 
 42: The weight difference $\Delta = \text{M1-iter} - \text{M1}$ was grafted onto M1 at ranks 1 to 16 (Equation~\ref{eq:recovery}), and the model was run iteratively. These are the CPU-trained models of the iterative-reranking experiment, so $\Delta$ here still includes the training-data confound addressed in Section~\ref{sec:res_matched}. Without the graft, M1 run iteratively reached recall@5 of 0.45 on the tuning set (\texttt{dev300}), against 0.74 for M1-iter. Recovery was 74\% at rank 1 and 95\% at rank 2, and stayed flat at higher ranks (97\% at ranks 4 and 8). A random rank-4 change of the same norm (one draw, tuning set) recovered 9\%. On the test set, the rank-2 graft recovered 84.7\% of the gain (CI $[73.6\%, 96.2\%]$); it remained below the full model (14 vs 31 discordant questions, $p=0.016$). Grafting single layers showed that layers 3 and 4 contribute most (43\% and 40\%), but layers 0--2 also contribute about 30\% each, a flatter profile than for the HotpotQA skill (A1 $-$ A0), where a rank-4 graft recovered 80\% of a token-based measure of the gain and the middle layers dominated. Ranks 2 to 8 differ by about two questions on \texttt{dev300}; the result is a plateau from rank 2, not a strict ordering.

 58: \section{The effect is concentrated on long chains}

 61: An exploratory reading of the matched control suggested that the gain was concentrated on four-hop questions. ===== 03_related_work.tex =====
  1: \chapter{Related Work}
  2: \label{chap:related}
  3: 
  4: This chapter positions the work against three lines of research: context selection for RAG, multi-hop retrieval, and the structure of fine-tuning updates. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarizes what is known and what this work adds.
  5: 
  6: \section{Context selection and compression for RAG}
  7: 
  8: Several methods reduce what the LLM reads. RECOMP \cite{xu2024recomp} trains compressors that summarize retrieved documents; LongLLMLingua \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua} prunes tokens; FILCO \cite{wang2023filco} filters context at the span level; Provence \cite{chirkova2025provence} prunes sentences with a trained cross-encoder; and CompAct \cite{yoon2024compact} compresses iteratively. Adaptive methods decide how much to retrieve: Self-RAG \cite{asai2024selfrag} and Adaptive-RAG \cite{jeong2024adaptiverag} use the LLM or a classifier to decide, DynamicRAG \cite{sun2025dynamicrag} learns the number of documents, and Adaptive-$k$ \cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} cuts the ranked list at the largest gap in similarity scores and adds a buffer of extra passages (five by default). Noise-Gate \cite{lavado2027noisegate}, the author's earlier work, selects paragraphs by embedding-similarity thresholds and reported 26--27\% fewer tokens on HotpotQA; in that study Adaptive-$k$ selected too few passages and lost answer quality. Listwise LLM rerankers \cite{sun2023rankgpt,pradeep2023rankzephyr} are accurate but far more expensive than a small cross-encoder.
  9: 
 10: Compared with this line, the present work does not propose a new compression mechanism. Its practical contribution is a careful cost accounting of a very small selector at matched recall and answer quality, and the finding that on harder multi-hop data the ranker, not the stopping rule, is the bottleneck.
 11: 
 12: \section{Multi-hop retrieval}
 13: 
 14: Multi-hop dense retrieval \cite{xiong2021mdr} and Baleen \cite{khattab2021baleen} retrieve iteratively, conditioning each step on what was found before. IRCoT \cite{trivedi2023ircot} interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning by an LLM, and EfficientRAG \cite{zhuang2024efficientrag} replaces some LLM calls with small models. Beam Retrieval \cite{zhang2024beamretrieval} is the closest method to the iterative reranker studied here: a cross-encoder scores each candidate together with the question and the passages already chosen, with beam search. It reports high support recall on MuSiQue with much larger encoders (DeBERTa base and large).
 15: 
 16: The iterative reranker in this work is therefore not a new method. What is new is (i) its evaluation at 22.7M parameters with a pair-matched control that separates the effect of conditioning from the effect of more training data, and (ii) the analysis of the weight change it requires.
 17: 
 18: \section{Structure of fine-tuning updates}
 19: 
 20: The low intrinsic dimension of fine-tuning \cite{li2018intrinsic,aghajanyan2021intrinsic} and the success of low-rank adapters \cite{hu2022lora} are well established. PHLoRA \cite{vasani2025phlora} extracts low-rank adapters from full fine-tuning checkpoints with truncated SVD, the same operation used here. Localization studies find that task skills concentrate in few parameters or layers \cite{panigrahi2023skilllocalization}, including the middle layers of a large reranker \cite{perlman2026emergence}. LoRA updates differ structurally from full fine-tuning \cite{biderman2024loralearnsless,shuttleworth2025illusion}, and rank measures of updates need a noise baseline before they are interpreted \cite{altaf2026noisebaseline}.
 21: 
 22: Behaviours, not only domains, can sit in very low-rank updates. Rank-1 adapters suffice to induce emergent misalignment in an LLM, and the induced direction transfers across fine-tunes \cite{turner2025modelorganisms,soligo2025convergent}. Reasoning gains from reinforcement learning can be recovered with tiny adapters, while supervised fine-tuning needs much larger updates \cite{morris2026tinylora,ye2026rlvrlowrank}. Task arithmetic transfers domain knowledge between rerankers \cite{braga2025taskarithmetic}.
 23: 
 24: Against this background, a low-rank fine-tuning update is not surprising in itself. The specific claim tested here is narrower: a \emph{retrieval behaviour} (using already-selected evidence in the query) in a small reranker is a rank-2 change, isolated with a pair-matched control and norm-matched random controls, and it pays off on long chains.

===== 04_method.tex =====
  1: \chapter{Materials and Methods}
  2: \label{chap:method}
  3: 
  4: \section{Data}
  5: 
  6: \textbf{HotpotQA} (distractor setting) is used with the same 400 test questions as \citeonline{lavado2027noisegate} and 1,000 disjoint development questions, fixed by identifier. Each question has ten candidate paragraphs, two of them supporting.
  7: 
  8: \textbf{MuSiQue-Ans} \cite{trivedi2022musique} is used for the multi-hop experiments. The official test labels are not public, so all evaluation sets are drawn from the labelled development split, disjoint from each other and fixed in advance:
  9: \begin{itemize}
 10:   \item a 400-question test set and a 300-question tuning set (\texttt{dev300}), used up to the matched-control experiment;
 11:   \item a \emph{fresh} set of all 1,017 development questions not used before (529, 322 and 166 two-, three- and four-hop), used for the confirmation experiment.
 12: \end{itemize}
 13: Training questions come from the MuSiQue training split: 1,000 questions for fine-tuning and further disjoint samples for fitting stopping rules and routers. Every split was written to a file and committed before any model was scored on it.
 14: 
 15: \section{Models}
 16: 
 17: All selectors are the same 22.7M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder; only the weights differ (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Training uses a pointwise binary cross-entropy loss: each training example is a (query, paragraph) pair labelled 1 if the paragraph is supporting and 0 otherwise. Fine-tuning on MuSiQue uses AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 16, one epoch and a maximum length of 512 tokens; when the input is too long, the query side is truncated (if a paragraph alone exceeds the limit, both sides are truncated).
 18: 
 19: \begin{table}[htbp]
 20: \centering
 21: \caption{Selectors. All share the MiniLM-L6 architecture (22.7M parameters).}
 22: \label{tab:models}
 23: \small
 24: \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
 25: \toprule
 26: Name & Training \\
 27: \midrule
 28: A0 & Off-the-shelf MS MARCO reranker (\texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \\

 36: 
 37: \textbf{The confound and its control.} M1-iter sees about 2.2 times as many training pairs as M1, so a comparison of the two mixes the effect of conditioning with the effect of more training. M1-ctrl removes this confound: it receives the same paragraphs, labels, order, number of steps and learning-rate schedule as M1-iter, and differs only in the query text. M1-iter and M1-ctrl were trained with three seeds each.
 38: 
 39: \section{Inference}
 40: 
 41: \textbf{One-shot.} Every paragraph is scored against the question and the list is sorted; this costs one pass per paragraph (about 20 on MuSiQue).
 42: 
 43: \textbf{Iterative.} For four greedy steps, the remaining paragraphs are scored against a query made of the question followed by the paragraphs selected so far (most recent first, separated by a special token), and the highest-scoring paragraph is selected. The remaining paragraphs are then ordered by their scores in the last step. This costs about $20+19+18+17 = 74$ passes per question.
 44: 
 45: \textbf{Adaptive stopping.} Instead of a fixed $k$, a logistic regression estimates, for each $k$, the probability that the top $k$ contains all supporting paragraphs, from features of the selector's scores (the sorted scores, the score at position $k$ and $k{+}1$, and their gap). The system sends the smallest $k$ whose probability exceeds a threshold $\tau$; $\tau$ is chosen on a tuning set as the value with the fewest tokens that reaches a recall target (0.90 or 0.95), and applied once to the test set.
 46: 
 47: \section{Delta grafting}
 48: 
 49: To study the structure of a fine-tuning update, the difference between two models is \emph{grafted}, partly, onto the first one. Given a base model with weights $\{W^{(b)}\}$ and a target model $\{W^{(t)}\}$, the delta of each weight matrix is $\Delta = W^{(t)} - W^{(b)}$. A rank-$r$ graft replaces every two-dimensional $\Delta$ by its truncated SVD $\Delta_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:svd}) and keeps one-dimensional parameters (biases, layer norms) in full. A layer graft adds the full delta of a single Transformer layer only. The effect of a graft $g$ is summarized by its \emph{recovery},
 50: \begin{equation}
 51: \mathrm{rec}(g) = \frac{R(g) - R(\text{base})}{R(\text{target}) - R(\text{base})},
 52: \label{eq:recovery}
 53: \end{equation}
 54: where $R$ is full-support recall@5 under the target's inference mode. As a control, each $\Delta_r$ is replaced by a random rank-$r$ matrix with the same Frobenius norm; if random matrices recovered as much, ``low-rank'' would be indistinguishable from ``any perturbation of that size''. The graft is chosen without looking at data, so the tuning set can be used to select the smallest rank with at least 80\% recovery, which is then confirmed once on a held-out set with a paired bootstrap confidence interval for the recovery ratio.
 55: 
 56: \section{Research protocol}
 57: \label{sec:protocol}
 58: 
 59: Every experiment was pre-registered: its question, data splits, metrics, decision rules and budget were committed to version control before any model was scored on evaluation data \cite{nosek2018preregistration}, and deviations were recorded. Each experiment has one primary test; exploratory results are reported as such and are not promoted to confirmed claims without a new registered test on unused data. Recent protocols also state the minimum detectable effect \cite{card2020power}. Because the project uses AI tools for coding, analysis and drafting, each protocol was reviewed before running by an independent model instructed to argue for the null hypothesis, every citation was checked against its official source, and results copied between machines were recomputed and compared. Code, protocols, results and a ledger of all claims are kept in the project repository.

  6: \section{A small selector saves most of the tokens (HotpotQA)}

 29: \section{Adaptive stopping saves further tokens}
 30: \label{sec:res_adaptive}
 31: 
 32: On HotpotQA, choosing between two and four paragraphs per question with a learned stopping rule saved 57 and 71 tokens per question against A1's threshold at recall targets 0.90 and 0.95 (CIs $[-71, -44]$ and $[-91, -53]$), with recall differences not significant. On MuSiQue, adaptive stopping beat the pre-registered fixed-$k$ policy of the iterative ranker at recall 0.95 ($-244$ tokens, CI $[-296, -192]$) but not at 0.90 ($-43$, CI $[-95, +9]$), and the iterative ranker's scores were not a better stopping signal than one-shot scores (a refuted secondary). Descriptively, adaptive stopping on the one-shot in-domain ranker reached recall 0.95 with 1,554 tokens and 20 passes per question, against 2,535 tokens for sending all paragraphs. Adaptive-$k$ \cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} with its default buffer of five paragraphs, applied to the same cross-encoder scores, spent the same tokens as the learned rule but recovered fewer complete chains (0.815 vs 0.868; exploratory, test set); without the buffer it cut after 3.7 paragraphs on average (recall 0.50). This agrees with its failure on cosine scores in \citeonline{lavado2027noisegate}.
 33: 
 34: \section{On MuSiQue, ranking quality limits cost}
 35: \label{sec:res_bottleneck}
 36: 
 37: With A1 (trained on HotpotQA), the top ten paragraphs contained all supporting paragraphs for only 68\% of MuSiQue questions, so no stopping rule limited to $k\le10$ could reach the planned recall targets; the pre-registered comparison was declared not testable. The results below, from later experiments, suggest that ranking quality is the main limiting factor. Fine-tuning A1 on 1,000 MuSiQue questions (M1) raised full-support recall@5 from 0.355 to 0.682, and reduced the tokens needed for recall 0.90 from 1,957 to 1,533. The iterative model M1-iter reached 0.750 (McNemar $p=0.0067$ against M1), with the largest gain on four-hop questions (0.58 vs 0.36). That comparison, however, was confounded by the amount of training data (Section~\ref{sec:res_matched}).
 38: 
 39: \section{The iteration change is functionally rank 2}
 40: \label{sec:res_rank}
 41: 
 42: The weight difference $\Delta = \text{M1-iter} - \text{M1}$ was grafted onto M1 at ranks 1 to 16 (Equation~\ref{eq:recovery}), and the model was run iteratively. These are the CPU-trained models of the iterative-reranking experiment, so $\Delta$ here still includes the training-data confound addressed in Section~\ref{sec:res_matched}. Without the graft, M1 run iteratively reached recall@5 of 0.45 on the tuning set (\texttt{dev300}), against 0.74 for M1-iter. Recovery was 74\% at rank 1 and 95\% at rank 2, and stayed flat at higher ranks (97\% at ranks 4 and 8). A random rank-4 change of the same norm (one draw, tuning set) recovered 9\%. On the test set, the rank-2 graft recovered 84.7\% of the gain (CI $[73.6\%, 96.2\%]$); it remained below the full model (14 vs 31 discordant questions, $p=0.016$). Grafting single layers showed that layers 3 and 4 contribute most (43\% and 40\%), but layers 0--2 also contribute about 30\% each, a flatter profile than for the HotpotQA skill (A1 $-$ A0), where a rank-4 graft recovered 80\% of a token-based measure of the gain and the middle layers dominated. Ranks 2 to 8 differ by about two questions on \texttt{dev300}; the result is a plateau from rank 2, not a strict ordering.

 44: \section{Pair-matched control}
 45: \label{sec:res_matched}
 46: 
 47: Figure~\ref{fig:matched} illustrates the design. With identical training pairs, M1-iter run iteratively beat M1-ctrl run one-shot by 0.040 recall@5 on the test set (0.7475 vs 0.7075, CI $[-0.005, 0.085]$, $p=0.11$): the pre-registered primary test failed. All three seed estimates were positive ($+0.040$, $+0.028$, $+0.033$; re-trainings evaluated on the same questions, not independent replications), but the primary test did not establish an overall gain; with a minimum detectable effect of about 0.07, it cannot exclude a small one either. Training on more pairs alone accounted for about 0.025 of the original gain (M1-ctrl vs M1, $p=0.013$, descriptive).

 56: The pre-registered secondary test asked whether the pure conditioning change, $\Delta = \text{M1-iter} - \text{M1-ctrl}$, is also low-rank.  58: \section{The effect is concentrated on long chains}
 59: \label{sec:res_long}
 60: 
 61: An exploratory reading of the matched control suggested that the gain was concentrated on four-hop questions. This was tested on 566 four-hop questions never used before (166 unused development questions and 400 unused training questions; ``fresh'' below means unused, not a new dataset). M1-iter (iterative) beat M1-ctrl (one-shot) by 0.055 recall@5 (0.500 vs 0.445, CI $[0.007, 0.102]$, $p=0.035$), positive in both sources but heterogeneous ($+0.139$, $p=0.006$, and $+0.020$, not significant) and positive for all three seeds. On all 1,017 fresh development questions (exploratory), the gain was about zero on two-hop questions, $+0.078$ on three-hop ($p=0.010$) and $+0.139$ on four-hop questions ($p=0.006$).
 62: 
 63: A cascade that runs the one-shot ranker for every question and the iterative one only for questions predicted to be four-hop did not help: it routed 29\% of questions, reached 0.702 recall@5 with 41.5 passes, against 0.693 for one-shot (20 passes) and 0.745 for always-iterative (74 passes), and failed both pre-registered tests. The router predicted hop count reasonably (four-hop recall 0.76, precision 0.43), but not the questions where iteration actually helps.
